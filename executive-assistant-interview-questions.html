Executive Assistant interview questions that actually work.

Most EA interview questions reward a rehearsed answer. These reveal how a candidate actually thinks, with what a strong answer sounds like next to a weak one.

By Lily Shippen Published 3 September 2026 6 min read

In short: most EA interview questions are too easy to prepare for. The ones that work put the candidate inside a real decision and ask for the reasoning. Below are the questions I lean on after thirteen years of recruiting Executive Assistants, grouped by what they reveal: how someone prioritises, reads a situation, communicates, and acts on their principal’s behalf. For each, listen for the reasoning, not the polish.

Ask most Executive Assistants “how do you manage a busy diary?” and you will get the answer they rehearsed on the way in. It will be fluent, sensible, and tell you almost nothing. The questions that separate a good hire from a wrong one do something different: they put the candidate inside a real decision and make them show their working.

Here are the ones worth your time, and what a strong answer sounds like against a weak one.

Questions that reveal how they prioritise

“Walk me through how you triaged your inbox this morning, and why in that order.” A strong candidate names a principle: who is blocked without them, what is irreversible if it slips, what needs the principal and what they can close themselves. A weak one says “I do the urgent ones first,” which is not a principle, it is a reflex.

“You are mid-task, three urgent things land in the same minute, and your principal is unreachable. What do you do first?” You are not testing the answer, you are testing whether they can reason under pressure without freezing or escalating everything. The candidate who asks one or two sharp clarifying questions and then commits is the one you want.

Questions that reveal judgement and discretion

“What would you never do without checking with your principal first?” This is one of the most revealing questions you can ask. A strong answer draws a clear line and explains it: they will act on their own judgement for most things, but never commit the principal’s money, reputation or calendar past a certain point without a check. “I would check everything” tells you they cannot operate independently. “I would handle it all” tells you they do not know where the line is.

“Tell me about a time you disagreed with your principal. What did you do?” Listen for managing up done well: they raised it privately, made the case once, and then either changed the decision or committed to it fully. The answer to avoid is the one where they either never push back, or never let it go.

Questions that reveal communication

“Draft a short reply, out loud, declining a meeting on your principal’s behalf. Firm, but without damaging the relationship.” This puts their tonal range on display in real time. A great EA can be warm and immovable in the same three sentences. A weaker one is either apologetic to the point of leaving the door open, or blunt enough to cost the principal a relationship.

Questions that reveal partnership

“Tell me about something you handled that your principal never knew about.” This surfaces the invisible work, the problem caught and closed before it reached the desk. Strong EAs have a dozen of these. If the candidate cannot think of one, they may be executing tasks rather than protecting the principal.

“What will your last principal miss most now that you have gone?” The answer tells you what they think their real value was. The best answers are rarely about admin. They are about calm, foresight, and trust.

How to run it

Whatever you ask, ask for the why every time, and do not accept the headline. When they give you the polished version, follow up with “and then what?” until you reach the reasoning underneath. Use one real scenario from your own world rather than a generic hypothetical, because a candidate cannot pre-rehearse your Tuesday. The point is not to catch anyone out. It is to hear how they think when the script runs out.

Common questions

What are the best interview questions for an executive assistant?
Scenario and reasoning questions beat competency clichés. Ask how they triaged a real inbox and why, what they would never do without checking, and to draft a firm-but-warm reply on the spot. Each reveals judgement rather than preparation.

What should I look for in an EA’s answers?
Reasoning, not polish. Listen for a clear prioritisation principle, a sense of where their autonomy ends, tonal range in how they communicate, and evidence of work they handled before it reached the principal.

How do you test an EA’s judgement in an interview?
Give them a genuine conflict, two things that cannot both happen, and ask what they do first and why. The reasoning matters far more than the choice.

Can interviews alone tell you if an EA is good?
They help if the questions are good, but you are still hearing what someone says they would do. Pairing the interview with a scenario-based assessment lets you see how they actually work before you hire.
